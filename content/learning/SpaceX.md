## Research Overview

SpaceX is a private American space company founded by Elon Musk in 2002. Its broad goal is to reduce launch costs and, over the long term, build transportation infrastructure that allows humans to operate beyond Earth. Today, SpaceX is developing reusable rockets, crewed spacecraft, satellite internet, and technologies for lunar and Mars exploration at the same time.

This is a short research report based on publicly available information as of May 22, 2026.

## Core Businesses

### 1. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy

SpaceX's current workhorse launch vehicle is Falcon 9. Falcon 9 is a two-stage rocket, and its first-stage booster can be recovered and reused. SpaceX's strategy is to reuse the most expensive part of the rocket repeatedly, lowering the cost of access to space.

Falcon Heavy is a heavy-lift launch vehicle made by combining three Falcon 9-derived cores. It is used for heavier satellites, government missions, and deep-space spacecraft launches.

### 2. Dragon Spacecraft

Dragon is SpaceX's spacecraft for sending cargo and crew to the International Space Station. It plays an important role in NASA's Commercial Crew Program and Commercial Resupply Services, and it is a representative example of the United States using a privately operated spacecraft for crew transportation to the ISS.

### 3. Starlink

Starlink is an internet service based on a low Earth orbit satellite network. It aims for lower latency than traditional geostationary satellite internet and targets areas with weak terrestrial connectivity, ships, aircraft, and mobile communications markets.

In January 2026, the U.S. Federal Communications Commission approved SpaceX's plan to deploy 7,500 additional second-generation Starlink satellites. This shows that Starlink is not just a launch customer for SpaceX, but a potential long-term revenue base.

### 4. Starship

Starship is a super-heavy, fully reusable launch system under development by SpaceX. It consists of the Super Heavy booster and the Starship spacecraft, and it is intended to transport people and cargo to Earth orbit, the Moon, Mars, and beyond.

Starship is still in development, but it is the most important project in SpaceX's long-term strategy. Large-scale Starlink deployment, NASA's Artemis lunar lander, and SpaceX's Mars transportation plans all depend on whether Starship succeeds.

## Recent Developments

SpaceX continues to maintain a high launch cadence through Falcon 9. SpaceX's official launch schedule includes Starlink missions, national security missions, commercial satellites, ISS resupply, and crewed spaceflight.

Starship development and test launches are taking place at Starbase in Texas. SpaceX uses Starbase as the development, manufacturing, testing, and launch site for Starship and Super Heavy.

NASA is using SpaceX's Starship HLS as a lunar lander candidate for the Artemis program. However, lunar lander development is technically difficult and carries schedule risk. A NASA inspector general report also identifies Human Landing System development delays and safety verification as major management risks.

## Strengths

- SpaceX has extensive operational experience applying reusable rockets to real commercial launches.
- It vertically integrates Falcon 9, Dragon, Starlink, and Starship, which helps accelerate development.
- Starlink gives SpaceX a recurring revenue source beyond launch services.
- SpaceX has a broad customer base across NASA, the U.S. government, and commercial customers.

## Risks

- Starship is still in a development phase that requires more testing and verification.
- As Starlink grows, SpaceX must continue managing issues around spectrum, orbital congestion, and interference with astronomical observations.
- NASA's Artemis schedule can be delayed by many elements beyond SpaceX, including spacesuits, Orion, SLS, and Gateway.
- Dependence on Elon Musk as an individual, along with reputation risk, can affect the company's image.

## Conclusion

SpaceX is less a simple rocket launch company and more a company building space transportation and satellite communications infrastructure together. In the short term, Falcon 9 and Starlink are the center of the business. In the long term, Starship is likely to determine the company's next stage of growth.

The key questions are twofold. First, can SpaceX reproduce with Starship the reusable operations it has already demonstrated with Falcon 9? Second, can Starlink operate a large satellite network reliably while producing enough profitability? The answers to these two questions will shape SpaceX's future value.
